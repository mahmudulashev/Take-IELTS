Organic food: why?

Today, many governments are promoting organic or natural farming methods that avoid the use of pesticides and other artificial products. The aim is to show that they care about the environment and about people's health. But is this the right approach?

A Europe is now the biggest market for organic food in the world, expanding by 25 percent a year over the past 10 years. So what is the attraction of organic food for some people? The really important thing is that organic sounds more 'natural'. Eating organic is a way of defining oneself as natural, good, caring, different from the junk-food-scoffing masses. As one journalist puts it: It feels closer to the source, the beginning, the start of things. The real desire is to be somehow close to the soil.

B Unlike conventional farming, the organic approach means farming with natural, rather than man-made, fertilisers and pesticides. Techniques such as crop rotation improve soil quality and help organic farmers compensate for the absence of man-made chemicals. As a method of food production, organic is, however, inefficient in its use of labour and land; there are severe limits to how much food can be produced. Also, the environmental benefits of not using artificial fertiliser are tiny compared with the amount of carbon dioxide emitted by transporting food (a great deal of Britain's organic produce is shipped in from other countries and transported from shop to home by car).

C Organic farming is often claimed to be safer than conventional farming - for the environment and for consumers. Yet studies into organic farming worldwide continue to reject this claim. An extensive review by the UK Food Standards Agency found that there was no statistically significant difference between organic and conventional crops. Even where results indicated there was evidence of a difference, the reviewers found no sign that these differences would have any noticeable effect on health.

D The simplistic claim that organic food is more nutritious than conventional food was always likely to be misleading. Food is a natural product, and the health value of different foods will vary for a number of reasons, including freshness, the way the food is cooked, the type of soil it is grown in, the amount of sunlight and rain crops have received, and so on. Likewise, the flavour of a carrot has less to do with whether it was fertilised with manure or something out of a plastic sack than with the variety of carrot and how long ago it was dug up. The differences created by these things are likely to be greater than any differences brought about by using an organic or non-organic system of production. Indeed, even some 'organic' farms are quite different from one another.

E The notion that organic food is safer than 'normal' food is also contradicted by the fact that many of our most common foods are full of natural toxins. Parsnips cause blisters on the skin of agricultural workers. Toasting bread creates carcinogens. As one research expert says: 'People think that the more natural something is, the better it is for them. That is simply not the case. In fact, it is the opposite that is true: the closer a plant is to its natural state, the more likely it is that it will poison you. Naturally, many plants do not want to be eaten, so we have spent 10.000 years developing agriculture and breeding out harmful traits from crops.'

F Yet educated Europeans are more scared of eating traces of a few, strictly regulated, man-made chemicals than they are of eating the ones that nature created directly. Surrounded by plentiful food, it's not nature they worry about, but technology. Our obsessions with the failings and prior mistakes of science, and our 'green' culture, have created an entirely irrational and incorrect belief that all birds, bees and butterflies are flying around in the free world and only man is making a mess of it. That is why something as consumerist as organic food appeals, ignoring the fact that it is supported by a very highly artificial and industrialised system of agriculture.

G The ultimate reality is that organic farming is an empty luxury. As rural writer Richard Body says, 'Our society has established a system of producing food that is both highly effective and inexpensive. That leaves people with time and money to do other things.' Ultimately, the organic farming movement is an expensive, self-indulgent exercise that solves few of the problems it claims to address.

Does Water Have Memory?

A  The practice of homoeopathy was first developed by the German physician Samuel Hahnemann. During research in the 1790s, Hahnemann began experimenting with quinine, an alkaloid derived from cinchona bark that was well known at the time to have a positive effect on the fever associated with malaria. Hahnemann started dosing himself with quinine while in a state of good health, and reported in his journals that his extremities went cold, he experienced palpitations, an 'infinite anxiety', a trembling and weakening of the limbs, reddening cheeks and thirst. 'In short,' he concluded, 'all the symptoms of relapsing fever presented themselves successively...' Hahnemann's main observation was that things which create problems for healthy people cure those problems in sick people, and this became his first principle of homoeopathy: simila similibus (with help from the same). While diverging from the principle of apothecary practice at the time - which was contraria contrariis (with help from the opposite) - the efficacy of simila similibus was reaffirmed by subsequent developments in the field of vaccinations.

B  Hahnemann's second principle was minimal dosing - treatments should be taken in the most diluted form at which they remain effective. This negated any possible toxic effects of simila similibus. In 1988 the French immunologist Jacques Benveniste took minimal dosing to new extremes when he published a paper in the prestigious scientific journal Nature in which he suggested that very high dilutions of the anti-lgE antibody could affect human basophil granulocytes, the white blood cells that contain histamine (involved in allergic reactions) and heparin (involved in thinning the blood). The marvel of Benveniste's findings was that in his dilutions there was no mathematical probability that a single molecule of the original antibody could remain. This led him to the conclusion that water had the ability to 'remember' the antibody long after it had disappeared.

C  As can be imagined, this was a highly controversial finding, and it was met with some incredulity from the international scientific community, who requested that the experiment be repeated in three other independent laboratories. The experiments were overseen by John Maddox, editor of Nature, who brought along an expert in statistics and a professional illusionist (who went by the pseudonym of James Randi) to ensure that the findings were completely free of conscious or unconscious bias. In this second experiment, Benveniste's findings were not replicated, but the resulting debate stimulated further research into the premise that water could retain a memory of things that had been dissolved in it.

D  Following on from Benveniste's work, a team of researchers from Queen's University in Belfast, Northern Ireland, directed by Professor Madeleine Ennis, began a series of experiments. In 1999, the team demonstrated that homoeopathic solutions of histamine actually worked. By this time, the researchers were beginning to fall into two camps: those who were starting to believe that a memory effect of water was a distinct scientific possibility, and those who believed that the effect could be explained by some other means. While scientists who belonged to the latter group claimed that the water-memory effect could be explained by the agglomeration of molecules, the scientists in the former camp began trying to figure out exactly how the memory might be retained.

E  A theory proposed in 2000 suggested that the memory in water depends on hydrogen bonds. Water has a highly dynamic structure, and a hydrogen bond only lasts for a fraction of a nanosecond before it breaks and reforms with other molecules. The researchers claimed that when an element is dissolved in water, the hydrogen bonds are altered. The bonds then retain the changes in their network even after the dissolved element has been diluted to the point where it no longer exists.

F  A second theory, first suggested in 2005, proposed that the molecules of a dissolved element act as a template for the water's molecules. The water's molecules take on a 'footprint' of the dissolved element's molecules and form a new nanostructure. Some scientists believe that this nanostructure can remain in place long after the molecules of the dissolved element have been removed.

G  A third theory was suggested by an international team of scientists in 2007. They proposed that the memory of water is a result of the 'quantum entanglement' of its molecules. Quantum entanglement is a phenomenon that occurs when two or more particles are linked together in a way that the state of one particle is immediately reflected in the state of the other, even if they are separated by a large distance. The researchers claimed that when an element is dissolved in water, its molecules become quantum entangled with the water's molecules. The water's molecules then retain this entanglement even after the dissolved element has been diluted out.

H  The debate over whether water has a memory is likely to continue for some time. However, the research that has been conducted so far suggests that there may be some scientific basis for the memory effect of water. Whether this effect can explain the efficacy of homoeopathy remains to be seen. But the possibility that water has a memory is an intriguing one, and it is a reminder that we still have much to learn about the natural world.

The origins of laughter

A  While joking and wit are uniquely human inventions, laughter certainly is not. Other creatures, including chimpanzees, gorillas and even rats, chuckle. Obviously, they don’t crack up at Homer Simpson or titter at the boss’s dreadful jokes, but the fact that they laugh in the first place suggests that sniggers and chortles have been around for a lot longer than we have. It points the finger at evolutionary roots of laughter way before humans arrived on the scene. What these animal species 'laugh' at may surprise you. If you tickle a chimpanzee, it goes 'pant-pant-pant'. Let a rat play with another rat, and it emits ultrasonic squeaks from a ticklish spot on its nape. If you tickle a gorilla, it will chuckle.

B  There is no doubt that laughing and smiling are different, yet closely related behaviours. Smiling is more than just a mild version of a laugh, though. It’s a very different signal, says Robert Provine, an evolutionary neurobiologist. 'There’s evidence that smiling evolved from the "bared teeth display" of our primate ancestors,' he says. 'This is a friendly expression.' Laughter, on the other hand, evolved from a different behaviour: the 'relaxed open-mouth display', or play face. 'This is the panting, "pant-pant-pant" of chimpanzees,' he says. 'It’s the sound of rough and tumble play.'

C  The evolution of laughter from the panting of play has had a profound effect on the way we laugh. Provine’s studies of laughter reveal that it is tightly tied to our breathing. We laugh when we exhale, in short staccato bursts. 'Chimpanzees don’t have the same breath control,' says Provine. 'They laugh with each inhalation and exhalation, so their laughter is breathless. Humans have adapted to produce a more continuous stream of laughter. We can chop up a single exhalation into a series of short "ha-ha-ha" sounds. This is the same breath control that allows us to speak.' Laughter, in other words, may have been a pre-adaptation for speech.

D  But if laughter is related to play and tickling, why do we laugh at jokes? Provine thinks that laughter has evolved to become a more general social signal. 'It’s a way of saying "I’m playing, I’m not attacking you",' he says. 'It’s a mechanism for building social bonds.' This explains why we laugh much more frequently when we’re with other people than when we’re alone. 'Laughter is a highly social behaviour,' says Provine. 'We’re 30 times more likely to laugh when we’re with other people than when we’re by ourselves.'

E  If laughter is a social signal, it makes sense that we should be able to laugh even when we don’t find something funny. 'Fake laughter is a real phenomenon,' says Provine. 'We often laugh simply to be polite, or to show agreement. This kind of laughter is often less intense and shorter than spontaneous laughter.' It’s also controlled by a different part of the brain. Spontaneous laughter is generated by the brainstem, a primitive part of the brain that controls basic bodily functions like breathing and heart rate. Fake laughter, on the other hand, is controlled by the cerebral cortex, the more recently evolved part of the brain that is responsible for conscious thought and decision-making.

F  This dual control of laughter may explain why it can be so difficult to suppress a laugh when something is genuinely funny. The brainstem is largely outside our conscious control, so when it receives the signal to laugh, it’s very hard to stop it. Fake laughter, on the other hand, is much easier to control. We can choose when and how to laugh, depending on the social context. 'Laughter is a complex behaviour that serves many different functions,' says Provine. 'It’s a way of building social bonds, a way of signaling that we’re playing, and a way of dealing with stress and tension. It’s also a powerful tool for communication, capable of conveying a wide range of emotions and intentions.'

G  In recent years, researchers have begun to investigate the potential health benefits of laughter. Some studies suggest that laughter can reduce stress, boost the immune system, and even relieve pain. 'Laughter is a form of internal jogging,' says William Fry, a psychiatrist at Stanford University. 'It exercises the cardiovascular system, respiratory system, and muscular system. It also releases endorphins, the body’s natural painkillers.' While more research is needed to fully understand the health benefits of laughter, there is no doubt that it makes us feel good. And in a world that can often be stressful and demanding, a good laugh is something we can all appreciate.
