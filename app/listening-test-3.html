Section 4. You will hear a student who is studying food science giving a presentation on a spice called saffron.

I have focused on the cultivation and production of saffron, an expensive bright yellow spice made from crocus flowers. Unlike most other species of crocus, the saffron crocus doesn't flower in February or March, but in October, when it produces fragrant lilac coloured flowers, which are harvested at that time. During the harvest, the flower petals are discarded and only the stigmas are commercially used. One person must pick for almost 400 hours to produce just one kilo of saffron. And amazingly, 31to produce just 50 grams, it takes about 14,000 individual stigmas.

After this painstaking harvesting, and to preserve the intensive taste, smell and colour of the product, 32the stigmas are subject to a process of drying, by which some 98% of their moisture content is removed. The highly prized spice is famous for the flavour and aroma it imparts, which are often compared to that of honey. If you want to buy some of this fabled spice, you will find that 33saffron is either sold as whole stigmas or ground up as a powder.

Today saffron is used in both eastern and western cuisines to add both colour and flavour to an enormous range of recipes, though cooks would agree that 34it is most commonly added to recipes for the cooking of rice, because somehow it seems to complement such dishes perfectly.

Although food preparation is its most common present-day use, medical research indicates that 35saffron may have important applications as a drug in the clinical treatment of a number of eye disorders. And although scientists are still unsure of exactly why, 36recent experiments with laboratory rats have shown saffron to be effective in protecting the animals from damage caused when they are exposed to potentially harmful lights.

Some of the earliest evidence of saffron cultivation comes from archaeological excavations dating back 4,000 years on the island of Crete, 37where paintings and other artifacts have been found indicating that women wore beautiful saffron-coloured clothes. This is the earliest evidence of saffron being used as a dye.

During the Roman Empire, 38saffron oil was produced, which, because of both its colour and aroma, was used to create cosmetics, but only the wealthiest citizens could afford these. Another use of saffron was to be found in the public baths. 39There, saffron was used as a perfume to keep the places smelling fresh and aromatic.

Other parts of the ancient world used saffron extensively. One of the most well-known examples was in Persia, which is modern-day Iran, 40where saffron was a vital part of the process of manufacturing carpets, being used as a traditional dye along with other natural products like berries and minerals.
